Climate Change Impact Assessment for Pinus palustris Mill.

Abstract

Background: Pinus palustris Mill. (Longleaf Pine) is a fire-dependent keystone species of southeastern United States ecosystems that has experienced severe population declines due to habitat loss, fire suppression, and land use changes. Climate change poses additional threats to remaining populations through changing temperature and precipitation patterns that may affect species survival and overall ecological community resilience.

Methods: I conducted MaxEnt-based habitat suitability modeling with occurrence data from GBIF (n=274 spatially filtered records) and ClimateNA-sourced climate projection data (SSP2-4.5 emissions scenario) across three time periods: current climate (1991-2020), mid-century (2041-2070), and late-century (2071-2100). This analysis incorporated traditional bioclimatic, topographic, and ecological predictors including fire regime suitability and canopy openness to capture the species' fire-dependent habitat requirements.

Results: The model achieved acceptable predictive performance (Training AUC = 0.747, Test AUC = 0.706) with canopy openness (35.7% contribution) and spring precipitation (20.3% contribution) as primary habitat predictors. Current suitable habitat covers 106,598 km² in Alabama. Climate projections indicate minimal habitat changes: +0.1% by mid-century and +2.9% by late-century, suggesting stable habitat availability under the SSP2.5-4 future climate scenario. Climate refugia analysis shows that 88.3% of current suitable habitat remains suitable across all three time periods, indicating high climate stability.

Conclusions: Pinus palustris habitat in Alabama shows moderate resilience to projected climate change, but conservation efforts should prioritize climate refugia areas and maintain fire management regimes (e.g. prescribed burning) critical to species persistence and ecological community health. Specifically, the integration of ecological variables improved model performance and captured ecological realism, demonstrating the importance of incorporating species-specific niche requirements in climate change assessments. Future efforts should focus on an expanded study area and scope encompassing the wider southeastern United States and other community members, using higher quality baseline data, including other relevant predictors, and implementing further spatial analysis.

1. Introduction

1.1 Species Background

Pinus palustris Mill., commonly known as Longleaf Pine, is a long-lived coniferous tree species endemic to the southeastern United States. Historically, longleaf pine ecosystems covered approximately 370,000 km² across its range, but current estimates suggest approximately 3% of this original extent remains (Frost, 1993; Ware et al., 1993; Landers et al., 1995; Frost, 2006). The species is characterized by its distinctive habit, adaptation to fire, and role as a keystone species supporting one of North America's most biodiverse ecosystems.

1.1.1 Pinus palustris Habitat and Community

Longleaf pine ecosystems represent fire-maintained savanna communities characterized by an open, widely-spaced canopy structure that historically dominated the southeastern United States landscape (Peet, 2006). These ecosystems are among the most biodiverse in North America, with the forest floor supporting remarkable plant diversity of up to 50 species per square meter (Walker & Peet, 1984; Kirkman et al., 2001). The community structure consists of a distinctive architecture: an overstory dominated by longleaf pine, a sparse or absent midstory maintained by frequent fire, and a diverse herbaceous understory layer typically dominated by wiregrass species (Aristida stricta and Aristida beyrichiana) (Peet, 2006). These wiregrass species play a critical ecological role in carrying the frequent, low-intensity surface fires essential for ecosystem maintenance (Walker & Peet, 1984). As a fire-climax community, the longleaf pine ecosystem depends on fire return intervals of 1-5 years to maintain its open savanna structure and prevent succession to closed-canopy forest (Boyer, 1990; Gilliam & Platt, 1999s).

1.2 Conservation Status and Threats

The dramatic decline of longleaf pine ecosystems represents one of the most significant conservation challenges in the southeastern United States. Primary threats include:

  • Habitat conversion: Agricultural development, urbanization, and conversion to pine plantations (Croker, 1987; Landers et al., 1995)
  • Fire suppression: Disruption of natural fire cycles essential for ecosystem health (Mitchell et al., 2006; Kirkman et al., 2017)
  • Fragmentation: Isolation of remaining populations and reduced landscape connectivity (Noss 1989).
  • Climate change: Potential shifts in temperature, precipitation, and fire weather patterns

1.3 Climate Change Implications

Climate change poses complex challenges for fire-dependent ecosystems. Projected increases in temperature and altered precipitation patterns may affect longleaf pine along multiple vectors:

  • Direct physiological stress from temperature and drought
  • Altered fire weather patterns affecting fire frequency and intensity
  • Shifts in competitive relationships with other plant species
  • Changes in regeneration timing and seedling success

1.4 Current Conservation Efforts

Many conservation efforts centered on Pinus palustris have been underway for decades, from organizations like The Longleaf Alliance.

Understanding how climate change may affect longleaf pine habitat suitability is essential for developing effective conservation strategies and informing restoration prioritization across longleaf pine's range.

1.5 Past research

1.5.1 Pinus palustris modeling

Habitat suitability modeling for longleaf pine has been limited, with most research focusing on dendrochronological studies, growth-climate relationships, and local habitat assessments rather than range-wide distribution modeling under climate change scenarios. The most comprehensive prior modeling effort is the USDA Forest Service Climate Change Tree Atlas (Version 4), which models suitable habitat for longleaf pine across the eastern United States using Random Forest methodology based on Forest Inventory and Analysis data and climate projections from three general circulation models (GCMs; CCSM4, GFDL, HadGEM2-ES) under RCP 4.5 and 8.5 emissions scenarios (Iverson et al., 2019). This analysis uses importance values derived from forest inventory and analysis (FIA) plots and incorporates 45 environmental predictors including climate, elevation, soil properties, and soil taxonomy variables, achieving "medium" model reliability for longleaf pine. The Tree Atlas projects substantial habitat expansion under climate change, with potential suitable area increasing from 4.9% to 29.2-36.9% of the study region under moderate and high emissions scenarios, respectively. However, this modeling approach does not incorporate fire regime variables or other disturbance factors specific to longleaf pine's fire-dependent habitat requirements.

1.5.2 Ecological Predictor Importance

Recent research on longleaf pine ecology has increasingly emphasized the critical importance of fire regimes and habitat structure in determining species distribution and ecosystem function. Dendrochronological studies have revealed complex climate-growth relationships, with longleaf pine showing sensitivity to summer precipitation and temperature variables, though these relationships exhibit non-stationary patterns through time (Zampieri & Pau, 2022). Additionally, recent work has demonstrated that climate conditions during the growing season have become more influential on longleaf pine growth than previous year's conditions, with trees showing reduced resilience to frequent and intense droughts that have occurred since the 2000s (Campôa & Puhlick, 2023).

The incorporation of species-specific ecological variables in habitat suitability modeling has gained recognition as a critical methodological advancement. Research demonstrates that models integrating ecologically relevant predictors beyond climate variables—such as habitat structure, disturbance regimes, and biotic interactions—consistently outperform traditional climate-only approaches (Hirzel & Le Lay, 2008). When a species is strongly affected by specific ecological processes, predictors should include variables that reflect these key ecological drivers rather than relying solely on broad climatic factors (Hirzel & Le Lay, 2008). Studies have shown that models integrating habitat-specific predictors with climatic variables achieve significantly higher performance than models using climate predictors alone (Dubuis et al., 2022). This approach is particularly important for species with specialized ecological requirements, where environmental variables should reflect key factors of the species' niche beyond basic climate, topography, and geology (Hirzel et al., 2006).

1.5.3 MaxEnt Modeling

While correlative approaches like MaxEnt relate species distributions to environmental variables statistically, mechanistic models attempt to incorporate physiological processes and demographic parameters to predict species responses (Kearney & Porter, 2009). However, correlative models remain the most widely applied approach for range-wide climate change assessments due to their data efficiency and ability to capture realized niche relationships at landscape scales (Elith & Leathwick, 2009), making them well-suited for conservation planning applications like this longleaf pine assessment.

Studies of related pine species have employed MaxEnt modeling with varying success, achieving AUC values ranging from 0.73 to 0.98. For example, species distribution modeling of Pinus pinaster in Portugal using MaxEnt achieved an overall accuracy of 74% and projected climate resilience with minimal habitat area changes under future scenarios (Ramos et al., 2023). However, these studies have typically focused on climate and topographic variables without incorporating the fire regime components that are fundamental to longleaf pine ecology.

The current study builds upon this foundation by integrating fire regime suitability and canopy openness variables specifically developed for longleaf pine's ecological requirements, employing contemporary MaxEnt methodology with systematic variable selection, and providing multi-temporal climate change projections using current climate scenarios. This approach addresses identified gaps in previous modeling efforts by incorporating the fire-dependent nature of longleaf pine ecosystems while maintaining rigorous statistical methodology for climate change impact assessment. Additionally, the current study focuses specifically on Alabama as a detailed case study rather than the broad eastern US scope of the Tree Atlas, allowing for more refined spatial resolution and state-specific conservation implications (however, it should be noted that the study area was partially defined by computational constraints).

2. Data & Methods

2.1 Study Area

The study was conducted across the state of Alabama, which encompasses approximately 135,760 km² total area (131,172 km² land area) and represents a significant portion of the longleaf pine's historical and current range (U.S. Census Bureau, 2023). Alabama's diverse physiographic regions, from the Ridge-and-Valley hills to the Gulf Coastal Plain, provide a comprehensive gradient of conditions for assessing habitat suitability patterns (Griffith, 2001). Open water and urbanized land cover classes were excluded from the analysis to focus modeling efforts on terrestrial habitats suitable for forest species colonization. Study area boundaries were obtained from the U.S. Census Bureau's Master Address File/Topologically Integrated Geographic Encoding and Referencing (MAF/TIGER) System (U.S. Census Bureau, 2023), providing Alabama state and county political boundaries for spatial analysis and data munging.

2.2 Occurrence Data

Species occurrence records were obtained from the Global Biodiversity Information Facility (GBIF) through manual data retrieval from their website (GBIF.org, 2025). The initial dataset contained occurrence records spanning 2003-2025, filtered to include only human-observed records and excluding herbarium specimens that might complicate accurate observation data. Data preprocessing included:

  • Spatial filtering with 2km minimum distance between points to reduce spatial autocorrelation
  • Coordinate validation and removal of records with high uncertainty
  • Geographic filtering to Alabama study area boundaries
  • Final dataset: 274 spatially filtered, high-quality occurrence records

Data Quality Control

Rigorous quality control procedures ensured occurrence data reliability, including coordinate validation, uncertainty thresholds, and systematic bias assessment through comparison with human-observed records.

Spatial Filtering

Applied 2km minimum distance filtering to reduce spatial autocorrelation while maintaining adequate sample size for model training and validation across environmental gradients. This distance reflects the limited dispersal ability of longleaf pine seeds, which typically disperse less than 1-2 tree lengths from parent trees under natural conditions.

2.3 Environmental Variables

2.3.1 Bioclimatic Variables

Climate variables were obtained from ClimateNA v7.30 (Wang et al., 2016), which provides high-resolution climate surfaces based on PRISM data (Daly et al., 2008) and downscaled climate model projections (Mahony et al., 2022). Variables were extracted for three time periods:

  • Current climate: 1991-2020 normals
  • Mid-century: 2041-2070 (13 GCM ensemble, SSP2-4.5)
  • Late-century: 2071-2100 (13 GCM ensemble, SSP2-4.5)

2.3.2 Ecological Variables

Ecological variables were developed to capture the species' fire-dependent habitat requirements and soil preferences:

Fire Regime Suitability

Data source: LANDFIRE Biophysical Settings Fire Return Interval (FRI_SURFAC), circa 2016 (LANDFIRE, 2016)

Processing: Fire return intervals were classified into suitability scores based on longleaf pine ecological requirements (Boyer, 1990; Gilliam & Platt, 1999; Frost, 2006):

  • FRI ≤ 5 years: Suitability = 95 (Optimal)
  • FRI 6-15 years: Suitability = 75 (Good)
  • FRI 16-30 years: Suitability = 55 (Moderate)
  • FRI 31-100 years: Suitability = 35 (Poor)
  • FRI >100 years: Suitability = 15 (Very Poor)

Canopy Openness

Data source: NLCD 2023 Annual Land Cover (Homer et al., 2015)

Processing: A multi-step workflow was implemented to convert NLCD forest classification to canopy openness metrics, based on fire-canopy relationships described by Mitchell et al. (2006):

  • Forest classes (41=Deciduous, 42=Evergreen, 43=Mixed Forest) were extracted
  • 3×3 focal window/neighborhood analysis calculated local forest density
  • Canopy openness derived as: 100 - (forest density × 100)
  • Cross-projection handling managed coordinate reference system differences between NLCD (NAD83) and ClimateNA (WGS84)
  • Final values represent percentage openness, where higher values indicate fire-maintained savanna conditions optimal for longleaf pine

Soil Variables

Data source: SoilGrids.org (Poggio et al., 2021; Turek et al., 2023)

Processing: Sand fraction (g/kg) data were retrieved and processed at three depths reflecting longleaf pine's preference for well-drained, sandy soils:

  • Surface layer: 0-5cm depth
  • Shallow subsurface: 15-30cm depth
  • Deep subsurface: 30-60cm depth

These variables were included in the initial variable set before the variable selection process to capture soil drainage characteristics critical for longleaf pine establishment and growth.

2.3.3 Topographic Variables

Topographic variables were derived from SRTM 30m resolution digital elevation data and processed through a multi-step workflow, described in Section 2.3.5 below, to ensure spatial consistency with climate data:

  • Elevation: Direct extraction from SRTM DEM
  • Slope: Calculated as percent rise using the terra R package
  • Aspect: Aspect converted to northness (cosine) and eastness (sine) components to handle circular nature of aspect data, also using the terra R package
  • Topographic Wetness Index (TWI): Calculated using slope-based proxy methodology

All topographic derivatives were calculated at native 30m resolution before resampling to 1km resolution using bilinear interpolation to match the ClimateNA template structure. A 5km buffer was applied around Alabama boundaries during processing to minimize edge effects during resampling operations.

2.3.4 Digital Elevation Model and Topographic Framework

The spatial framework for all environmental variables was established using Shuttle Radar Topography Mission (SRTM) digital elevation data (Farr et al., 2007). The SRTM DEM provided the baseline 30m resolution raster structure that was subsequently resampled to 1km resolution to match ClimateNA extremely strict software requirements. All topographic derivatives (elevation, slope, aspect components, and topographic wetness index) were calculated from this SRTM foundation before alignment with the ClimateNA template structure.

2.3.5 Spatial Data Integration and Quality Assurance

All environmental variables underwent systematic processing to ensure spatial consistency and alignment:

  • Template Establishment: ClimateNA ASCII files served as the master spatial reference, establishing consistent 1km resolution, extent, and coordinate reference system (WGS84) across all variables datasets.
  • Multi-source Integration:> Variables from different sources (LANDFIRE, NLCD, SoilGrids) required careful reprojection and resampling procedures to adhere to the ClimateNA raster structure:
    • Native resolution (30m → 1km) processing, followed by template alignment
    • Bilinear interpolation processing followed by template alignment
    • Nearest neighbor resampling for categorical data
    • 5km buffer zone to minimize edge artifacting
  • Quality Control: Comprehensive validation ensured spatial consistency, precision and accuracy:
    • Study area extent verification across all raster layers
    • Resolution-matching confirmation
    • Coordinate reference system alignment
    • Pixel value range validation to ensure bioclimatic and ecological realism

2.4 Variable Selection

An automated multi-criteria variable selection approach was implemented to optimize model performance while minimizing multicollinearity. From an initial set of 91 ClimateNA variables plus ecological enhancements, variables were systematically reduced to an optimal subset using multiple methodological criteria.

2.4.1 Selection Framework

Three candidate variable sets were developed representing different ecological prioritization strategies:

  • Enhanced literature-prioritized: Variables selected based on longleaf pine ecological literature with priority given to fire-related variables
  • Enhanced PCA-informed: Variables selected based on principal component loadings explaining 85% of environmental variance
  • Enhanced balanced ecological domains: Variables ensuring representation across habitat structure, fire-climate, soil drainage, and core climate domains

2.4.2 Multi-Criteria Scoring System

Each candidate set was evaluated using a weighted composite scoring system based on established methodological standards:

  • VIF analysis (40% weight): Variance Inflation Factor threshold of 10 (Dormann et al., 2013)
  • Correlation assessment (25% weight): Variables with |r| > 0.8 penalized
  • Sample size optimization (15% weight): Target ratio of 10-15 samples per variable
  • Performance validation (15% weight): Cross-validated GLM AUC assessment
  • Ecological enhancement bonus (5% weight): Priority for fire-dependent habitat variables

2.4.3 Final Variable Set

The enhanced balanced ecological domains approach achieved the highest composite score and was selected for final modeling. The final model included 8 variables representing all major ecological domains:

Selected Variables and VIF Scores

  • Fire Regime Suitability | VIF: 1.26
  • Canopy Openness | VIF: 2.54
  • Sand Content (0-5cm) | VIF: 2.35
  • Topographic Wetness Index | VIF: 2.73
  • Mean Annual Temperature | VIF: 7.41
  • Spring Precipitation | VIF: 7.74
  • Climatic Moisture Defecit | VIF: 11.39
  • Mean Annual Precipitation | VIF: 27.09

Note: While MAP exceeded the VIF threshold of 10, it was retained due to its fundamental ecological importance for longleaf pine and acceptable performance in cross-validation testing.

This systematic approach ensured optimal balance between model parsimony, statistical validity, and ecological realism while maintaining the fire-dependent habitat enhancements critical for longleaf pine modeling.

2.5 Species Distribution Modeling

2.5.1 MaxEnt Configuration

MaxEnt modeling was conducted using the following optimized parameters derived from systematic parameter testing across 0.5, 1.0, 1.5, 2.0, and 3.0 regularization multipliers for Linear, Linear Quadratic, Linear Quadratic Hinge, and Linear Quadratic Hinge Product feature types:

  • Features: Linear, Quadratic, Hinge, Product (LQHP)
  • Regularization multiplier: 1.5
  • Background points: 4,350 random samples
  • Model tuning: 5-fold cross-validation
  • Final validation: 10-fold cross-validation
  • Output format: Cloglog (complementary log-log)

2.5.2 Bias Correction

As this study relied on presence-only occurrence data, necessary steps had to be taken to account for sampling bias. This type of error is introduced to study designs by the inherent nature of collecting this type of citizen science data--observations are far more frequently logged in easily-accessible locations, such as recreational areas or rights-of-way. Sampling bias was addressed through a bias surface derived from 209,496 human-observed Plantae records, controlling for accessibility and observer effort patterns across the landscape (GBIF.org, 2025).

2.5.3 Model Evaluation

Model performance was evaluated using multiple metrics following established SDM evaluation protocols (Phillips et al., 2006; Elith et al., 2011):

  • Area Under the Curve (AUC) of Receiver Operating Characteristic
  • 10th percentile training presence threshold
  • Variable contribution and permutation importance
  • Jackknife tests of variable importance

Progressive Model Improvement

I iterated upon this analysis many times over, testing out functionality at every step along the way. After establishing a working design solely using the ClimateNA bioclimatic and DEM-derived topographic variables, I expanded the scope of the study to include ecological predictors. The enhancement of model performance was apparent after initial runs, pushing the Test AUC over the critical 0.7 threshold. Crucially, I was able to add a dimension of ecological realism that was not included in my earlier tests, as evidenced by the 35.7% relative importance of canopy openness to the model. This enhancement demonstrates the importance of incorporating species-specific ecological requirements in habitat suitability modeling.

3.3 Current Habitat Distribution

Under current climate conditions (1991-2020), the model predicts approximately 106,598 km² of suitable longleaf pine habitat across Alabama, representing 81.3% of the state's land area. This extensive suitable habitat indicates that climate conditions alone are favorable for longleaf pine across most of Alabama, with habitat limitations likely driven by other factors such as fire suppression, land use changes, and soil conditions in specific locations.

  • Gulf Coastal Plain: Extensive suitable areas in southern Alabama
  • Wiregrass Region: High-quality habitat in southeastern counties
  • Black Belt Prairie margins: Scattered suitable areas along ecotone boundaries
  • Fall Line Hills: Fragmented suitable habitat in transitional zones

4. Discussion

4.1 Model Performance and Innovation

The ecology-enhanced MaxEnt model demonstrated good predictive performance (Training AUC = 0.747, Test AUC = 0.706), representing an advancement in longleaf pine habitat modeling through the incorporation of ecological variables. The inclusion of canopy openness, fire regime suitability, and soil variables addressed a critical gap in previous species distribution models for fire-dependent species (Franklin, 2010). The high contribution of canopy openness (35.7%) validates the importance of fire-maintained habitat structure, while the relatively low contribution of fire regime suitability (2.8%) suggests that current fire suppression has homogenized fire patterns across the landscape.

4.2 Ecological Insights and Temporal Variable Dynamics

The changing variable importance patterns across time periods reveal critical ecological dynamics of longleaf pine response to climate change (Outcalt, 2000; Jose et al., 2006; Kirkman & Jack, 2017):

4.2.1 Structural Habitat Requirements

Canopy openness dominance: The persistent dominance of canopy openness (35.7% → 28.8% → 39.0%) confirms the species' absolute requirement for fire-maintained savanna structure. The U-shaped temporal pattern suggests initial mid-century vulnerability followed by late-century recovery, possibly indicating a lag period where fire management becomes increasingly critical as other environmental stresses intensify.

4.2.2 Shifting Moisture Relationships

Spring precipitation decline: The dramatic decline in spring precipitation importance (20.3% → 8.6% → 4.4%) suggests fundamental shifts in seasonal moisture relationships. This pattern indicates that spring moisture, currently critical for seedling establishment and early growth, becomes less limiting under future conditions—possibly due to enhanced winter precipitation or shifted phenology.

Annual precipitation emergence: Conversely, mean annual precipitation shows a mid-century spike (3.5% → 13.0% → 9.8%), suggesting a transitional period where total moisture availability becomes temporarily critical before other factors (likely temperature-driven) assume greater importance.

4.2.3 Edaphic Factor Intensification

Soil drainage amplification: Sand content shows peak importance in mid-century (18.7% → 29.7% → 27.1%), indicating that soil drainage becomes increasingly critical during the transition period. This pattern suggests that well-drained soils provide crucial buffering against moisture stress during climate transition, with sandy soils offering both drainage benefits and potentially better thermal regulation.

4.2.4 Emerging Drought Stress

Climatic moisture deficit progression: The consistent increase in CMD importance (1.5% → 3.0% → 4.0%) reveals emerging drought stress as a gradually intensifying constraint. This monotonic increase suggests cumulative moisture stress effects that compound over time, requiring enhanced physiological tolerance and potentially favoring populations with drought adaptation.

4.3 Model Quality and Temporal Stability

The temporal changes in variable importance provide insights into model quality and ecological realism. The consistent dominance of canopy openness across all periods validates the model's ecological foundation, while the coherent patterns of change in moisture-related variables (spring precipitation declining as CMD increases) suggest the model is capturing meaningful climate-vegetation relationships rather than spurious correlations.

The mid-century peak in several variables (sand content, mean annual precipitation, fire regime suitability) indicates a transitional period where multiple environmental factors become co-limiting. This convergent pattern suggests heightened ecological sensitivity during the 2041-2070 period, where habitat quality may become more dependent on the intersection of multiple favorable conditions. The return to more simplified variable relationships in late-century (with canopy openness and sand content dominating) suggests either adaptation to new conditions or threshold effects where surviving populations occupy increasingly specialized niches.

4.4 Climate Change Implications

The relatively modest habitat changes projected under climate change (+0.1% to +2.9%) suggest that longleaf pine may be more resilient to direct climate effects than previously anticipated. However, this apparent resilience must be interpreted within the context of existing threats:

4.4.1 Direct Climate Effects and Adaptation Signatures

The variable importance trajectories suggest longleaf pine may undergo adaptive shifts in response to changing climate conditions. The declining importance of spring precipitation coupled with increasing CMD suggests a transition from spring moisture limitation to growing season drought stress. This shift implies that successful populations may increasingly depend on:

  • Enhanced drought tolerance mechanisms
  • Deeper root systems exploiting year-round moisture
  • Altered phenology reducing spring moisture dependence
  • Increased reliance on well-drained soil microsites

4.4.2 Fire Regime Transitions

The mid-century peak in fire regime suitability importance (2.8% → 6.1% → 1.6%) suggests a critical transitional period where fire management becomes especially important. This temporal pattern may reflect:

  • Changing fire weather patterns that temporarily increase fire regime sensitivity
  • Heightened vulnerability during climate transition requiring optimal fire conditions
  • Potential fire-climate interactions that peak during mid-century warming
  • Late-century adaptation where fire dependence stabilizes under new climate conditions

4.5 Conservation Implications

4.5.1 Temporal Conservation Priorities

The changing variable importance patterns suggest that conservation strategies should adapt over time:

  • Current-2040: Maintain spring moisture availability and canopy openness through fire management
  • 2041-2070: Prioritize well-drained soils and comprehensive fire regime management during peak vulnerability
  • 2071-2100: Focus on drought-adapted populations and simplified habitat requirements

4.5.2 Site Selection Criteria

Variable importance trends inform adaptive site selection for restoration and protection:

  • Immediate priority: Sites with high canopy openness and reliable spring moisture
  • Future resilience: Well-drained sandy soils with flexible fire management potential
  • Long-term refugia: Areas combining structural openness, soil drainage, and climate stability

4.6 Methodological Overview

This study demonstrates several methodological approaches to species distribution modeling:

  • Ecological integration: Incorporation of fire regime and soil variables in SDM
  • Automated variable selection: Multi-criteria optimization approach
  • Bias surface development: Species-specific bias correction methodology
  • Multi-temporal analysis: Comprehensive climate change impact assessment

5. Conclusions

This comprehensive climate change impact assessment for Pinus palustris in Alabama provides several key insights for conservation planning and species management:

5.1 Key Findings

  • Model performance: Ecology-enhanced MaxEnt modeling achieved good predictive performance (Test AUC = 0.706), validating the importance of incorporating species-specific ecological requirements
  • Habitat stability: Projected climate change impacts are relatively modest (+0.1% to +2.9%), suggesting direct climate tolerance resilience
  • Temporal ecological dynamics: Variable importance patterns reveal three distinct phases: current spring moisture dependence, mid-century multi-factor vulnerability, and late-century drought adaptation
  • Fire dependence evolution: Canopy openness maintains dominance (35.7% → 28.8% → 39.0%) with mid-century dip suggesting temporary vulnerability during climate transition
  • Moisture stress transition: Declining spring precipitation importance (20.3% → 4.4%) coupled with increasing climatic moisture deficit (1.5% → 4.0%) indicates fundamental shifts in seasonal moisture relationships
  • Soil drainage amplification: Sand content peaks in mid-century (29.7%) highlighting critical buffering role during climate transition
  • Climate refugia identified: 88.3% of current suitable habitat represents stable refugia areas prioritized for conservation
  • Regional variation: Central Alabama shows greatest vulnerability, while southeastern counties demonstrate highest resilience

5.2 Conservation Recommendations

  1. Prioritize climate refugia: Focus conservation resources on the 94,126 km² of stable habitat areas, particularly in southern and southeastern Alabama
  2. Implement adaptive fire management: Expand prescribed fire programs with temporal flexibility—maintaining current regimes through 2040, intensifying management during mid-century vulnerability (2041-2070), and adapting to simplified late-century requirements
  3. Target well-drained soils: Prioritize sandy soil sites for restoration and protection, especially during the critical mid-century transition period when soil drainage becomes increasingly important
  4. Enhance connectivity: Develop habitat corridors linking climate refugia to isolated populations, focusing on pathways that maintain soil drainage and fire management potential
  5. Monitor drought adaptation: Establish long-term monitoring to track emerging drought stress responses and identify populations developing enhanced drought tolerance
  6. Seasonal management adaptation: Shift from spring moisture conservation strategies toward growing season drought mitigation as seasonal moisture relationships change
  7. Adaptive management framework: Implement flexible conservation strategies that can respond to the three-phase ecological transition revealed by variable importance patterns

5.3 Future Research Directions

This study opens several avenues for future research. The overall project could be improved through expanded study area extent and higher resolution (beyond the 1 km used here), factors which were limited due to computational constraints of using a personal workstation. Future work could integrate more detailed data, such as soils from SSURGO and more up-to-date LANDFIRE data. The input DEM could be LiDAR-derived, which could produce higher-quality derivatives, like TWI, slope, and aspect. Additional research directions include:

  • Dynamic fire modeling: Integration of climate-fire models to project fire regime changes
  • Genetic adaptation analysis: Assessment of local adaptation and migration potential
  • Ecosystem-level modeling: Expansion to include associated species and community dynamics
  • Restoration prioritization: Development of spatially explicit restoration planning tools
  • Scenario expansion: Analysis under additional emission scenarios and time horizons

5.4 Broader Implications

The temporal variable importance analysis demonstrates that species distribution models can reveal not just where species may persist, but how their ecological relationships may evolve under climate change. The three-phase pattern observed—current dependencies, transitional vulnerability, and adapted requirements—suggests that climate change impacts on species may be more dynamic and temporally structured than typically captured in static habitat projections.

The methodological approaches demonstrated in this study, particularly the integration of ecological variables and temporal variable importance analysis, have broader applicability to species distribution modeling for fire-dependent species worldwide. The revelation that variable importance can shift dramatically across time periods (spring precipitation declining from 20.3% to 4.4%, while sand content peaks at 29.7% mid-century) suggests that conservation planning must account for temporal changes in species-environment relationships, not just spatial habitat shifts.

The relative resilience of longleaf pine to direct climate effects, combined with the species' dependence on fire management and evolving ecological relationships, underscores the complexity of climate adaptation. Conservation success will depend not only on protecting current habitat but also on anticipating how species-environment relationships may evolve, particularly during critical transition periods where multiple factors become co-limiting. The mid-century vulnerability window identified through variable importance patterns highlights the need for intensified management during climate transition periods.
